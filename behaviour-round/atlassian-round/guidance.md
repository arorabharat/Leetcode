Based on your recruiter call, the Atlassian Manager Round is primarily about proving that you operate at a Senior Software Engineer level — not just that you can design and code systems, but that you can own large projects, lead other engineers, make sound technical decisions, and deliver measurable business impact.

The recruiter explicitly mentioned four pillars for the Manager Round. Below is your preparation checklist, based on the transcript.

# 1. What you need to prepare

## 1. Owning Outcome

Highest priority — end-to-end project ownership

Prepare stories demonstrating that you:

- Owned a project from inception through production launch.
- Translated business and customer needs into technical solutions.
- Defined project scope, estimates, timelines, milestones and deliverables.
- Broke a large project into incremental releases.
- Delegated tasks and coordinated multiple engineers or teams.
- Managed cross-team dependencies and stakeholder expectations.
- Identified risks early and unblocked delivery.
- Handled scope changes and communicated delays or trade-offs.
- Owned production readiness, resilience, maintainability and extensibility.
- Measured the project's impact on team and organizational goals.

## 2. Operational Excellence

Improving reliability, quality and efficiency

Prepare stories demonstrating that you:

- Proactively identified a problem without waiting for an assignment.
- Improved system performance, reliability, scalability or engineering productivity.
- Used data, dashboards, logs and metrics to identify bottlenecks.
- Defined measurable success criteria before making improvements.
- Applied engineering best practices and explored better tools or technologies.
- Considered documentation, observability, analytics and operational support.
- Collaborated with other teams to improve engineering standards.
- Influenced others to adopt a better technical or operational approach.

Be ready with numbers: latency, throughput, error rate, incidents, cost, deployment time or developer productivity.

## 3. Leadership Ability

Technical leadership without relying on authority

Prepare stories demonstrating that you:

- Led the technical direction of a complex project.
- Made decisions under ambiguity or incomplete information.
- Compared alternatives and explained technical trade-offs.
- Influenced engineers and stakeholders who initially disagreed.
- Took calculated risks instead of always choosing the familiar solution.
- Adapted plans when requirements, priorities or constraints changed.
- Guided engineers through difficult technical problems.
- Used lessons from previous projects to make better decisions.
- Led initiatives across teams, ideally with organization-wide impact.

Be prepared to explain why you chose your approach, what alternatives you rejected, and what you would change today.

## 4. Organizational Impact

Impact beyond your own project and assigned tasks

Prepare stories demonstrating that you:

- Mentored engineers and helped them become more independent.
- Delegated meaningful ownership to help others grow.
- Proactively identified skill gaps and created mentorship plans.
- Improved onboarding or knowledge sharing.
- Organized technical discussions, brown-bag sessions or documentation.
- Introduced engineering practices adopted by the wider team.
- Created an environment where people could ask questions and disagree safely.
- Shared failures and lessons openly.
- Helped other engineers or teams deliver outcomes they could not easily achieve alone.

# 3. Structure every answer using STAR

Use this structure to prepare your stories. The recruiter emphasized that the interviewer will drill into how you actually executed the work, not just what you achieved.

S

Situation

What was the business problem? Why did it matter? What was the system's scale and complexity?

T

Task

What were you personally accountable for? What were the goals, constraints, timeline and dependencies?

A

Action — spend the most time here

Explain the technical design, alternatives considered, project breakdown, delegation, stakeholder alignment, risks, trade-offs and execution decisions. Clearly distinguish your contribution from the team's work.

R

Result and reflection

Give measurable business and technical outcomes, production impact, lessons learned, and what you would improve if you did it again.

For each story, be able to answer:

- How many engineers and teams were involved?
- What was the scale of the system and the complexity?
- How did you estimate the effort and decide milestones?
- What alternatives did you consider, and why did you reject them?
- What went wrong during execution?
- How did you resolve disagreements or unblock dependencies?
- What metrics improved, and by how much?
- What happened after launch?
- What would you do differently now?

# 4. Prepare for failure and difficult situations

The recruiter explicitly said that failures are expected and that claiming you have never failed is a red flag.

Prepare at least one strong story for each of these:

- A project that missed its original deadline or scope.
- A technical decision you made that turned out to be wrong.
- A production incident or reliability issue.
- A disagreement where the other person was right and you changed your mind.
- A disagreement where you successfully influenced the other team.
- A change in requirements that forced you to revise your plan.
- A mistake that taught you something you subsequently applied.
- A situation where a teammate was struggling and you helped them succeed.

Important: Don't blame another engineer, manager or organization. Explain what you owned, how you handled the situation, and what changed because of the experience.

# 5. Prepare the Atlassian Values Round too

Although you asked about the Manager Round, the recruiter stressed that Atlassian's values are also assessed in the Manager Round. Prepare the five official values:

- Play, as a team — collaboration, shared ownership and constructive disagreement.
- Build with heart and balance — empathy, thoughtful decisions and sustainable execution.
- Don't #@!% the customer — protecting customer trust and prioritizing customer needs.
- Open company, no bullshit — transparency, honesty and direct communication.
- Be the change you seek — proactive improvement, initiative and accountability.

Expect scenario-based questions rather than direct questions about values. For example, you may be asked how you handled a disagreement, responded to a mistake, supported a struggling teammate or improved a process that nobody had asked you to change.

# 6. What Atlassian considers red flags

Use this as a final self-review checklist.

### Readiness self-check

0/10

I cannot explain how my project supported business or organizational goals.

I describe only my coding tasks, not end-to-end ownership.

I cannot explain project scale, complexity, dependencies or metrics.

I cannot discuss production operations, reliability or maintainability.

I blame others when something goes wrong.

I claim I have never failed or cannot identify a lesson learned.

I wait for tasks instead of proactively identifying problems.

I resist changes or cannot explain how I handled ambiguity.

I describe mentoring as one-way instruction without follow-up or measurable growth.

I say I influenced others but cannot explain how I built alignment.

# 7. Your preparation plan

If you have limited time before the interview, prioritize in this order:

1. Pick your two strongest end-to-end projects. Know the business context, architecture, scale, milestones, dependencies, risks and production outcomes in depth.
2. Prepare 10–15 STAR story outlines. Focus on the decisions and actions you personally took, not polished scripts.
3. Quantify the impact. Know the before-and-after metrics and be honest about what you can substantiate.
4. Prepare failure and conflict stories. Show accountability, empathy, sound judgment and learning.
5. Prepare mentoring and organizational-impact stories. Demonstrate that your impact extends beyond your own code.
6. Rehearse follow-up questions aloud. Be ready for the interviewer to drill into the smallest technical or execution detail.
7. Review operational excellence. Be able to explain monitoring, incidents, resilience, deployment, rollback, maintainability and post-launch ownership.

The recruiter also specifically said to choose projects that are large in scope, high in scale and complexity, long-running, and already in production. You should not rely exclusively on short, isolated feature implementations.

## My recommendation

For your Senior SWE interview, the biggest risk is preparing technically strong project descriptions that don't adequately show leadership, planning, stakeholder management or organizational impact.

For each of your two flagship projects, prepare a complete narrative covering:

- Why the project mattered to the business.
- How you designed and broke down the work.
- How you led other engineers and coordinated teams.
- The hardest technical decision and its trade-offs.
- A major challenge, risk or failure.
- How you measured success.
- What you owned after launch.
- What you learned and would improve.

That will give you a strong foundation for most of the Manager Round, while your remaining stories cover mentoring, conflict, operational improvements and failures.
